Where the models ended up

Round 1 · picks 1–30

  • Expected value always falls with pick. The curve is built from positive gaps between adjacent picks, so pick 1 beats pick 2 in every posterior draw. A power law sets the typical gap and a centered random walk lets each gap move away from it.
  • Outcomes are right-skewed. Each player outcome is a Normal plus an Exponential upside (ex-Gaussian), parameterized so the slot mean is exact.
  • Risk changes smoothly. Outcome spread and skew follow second-order random walks: the slope drifts slowly, so the band can narrow or level off but cannot zigzag around a few careers.
  • Held-out RMSE 7.78, mean bias −0.02, band coverage 79–81% (target 80%).

Round 2 · picks 31–60

  • Hurdle. Whether a second-rounder logs any NBA minutes is modeled first; non-players are exactly zero.
  • Expected value always falls with pick, built from pooled positive gaps as in Round 1.
  • Played outcomes split into two groups. Fringe players sit near replacement; contributors add an exponential upside. The fringe level is derived so the played mean stays exact.
  • Each slot resembles its neighbors. Contributor share, upside and fringe spread follow per-pick random walks whose flexibility is learned from the data.
  • Held-out log density −2.30 (best of 6), mean bias −0.07.

The starting problem

The dashboard's 10th–90th player-outcome band had become jagged after the move from Win Shares to xRAPM. The cause was a workaround, not the data. The Gaussian likelihood fit the slot means well but could not describe skewed careers. So outcomes were simulated as the slot mean plus a real residual from a player drafted within ±2 picks.

That window is a hand-picked bandwidth with hard edges. One star or bust drafted at pick 9 shapes the band from picks 7 to 11, then vanishes at pick 12. With about 100 residuals per window, the 10th and 90th percentiles are noisy, and nothing smooths them across picks.

Iterations, in order

  1. Diagnosed the ±2-pick residual window finding

    Band edge roughness (mean absolute second difference) was 1.2–1.5 wins. Replacing the window meant putting the outcome shape inside the Stan model so every pick's spread and skew is pooled with its neighbors, with the pooling learned from the data.

  2. Restricted the fit to the 2015–2022 draft classes adopted

    Your call: the game has changed, and xRAPM's tracking inputs only exist from the mid-2010s. That leaves about 8 players per slot; the Bayesian models carry the extra uncertainty into every curve and simulation.

  3. Round 1: ex-Gaussian with a strictly decreasing mean adopted

    Two mean curves were tested: a pure power law and the pooled-gap version. Both tied the Gaussian on held-out error and beat it on held-out log density. The late-pick skew pressed against a 0.95 cap; raising it to 0.99 cut held-out bias from −0.21 to −0.07. Pooled gaps were adopted because they match the "each pick learns from its own history and its neighbors" design at no cost.

  4. Round 2: ex-Gaussian hurdle rejected after rebuild

    After the first full rebuild, Round 2 averaged 0.61 wins per pick against 0.80 observed. Played second-rounders bunch tightly just below zero, and a few are worth 18–25 wins. One exponential tail cannot fit both, so the model shrank the upside and the mean fell with it.

  5. Round 2: fringe/contributor mixture adopted, later refined

    It restored expected value to the old Gaussian's level (0.71–0.75 wins per pick in-sample) and fit better. The first prototype, with per-pick random walks on contributor share and upside, had 4 divergences. Switching those to log-linear trends still left 8 at adapt_delta 0.99, and a smaller step size (0.999) removed them. The first CV then showed a mean bias of −1.02. That turned out to be one chain stuck at its random starting point (step size about 1e-6). Starting chains near the prior means fixed it, and the clean CV gave a bias of −0.05.

  6. Investigated why the bands looked the way they did finding

    The Round 1 narrowing around picks 11–19 sits in different places in 1996–2014 and 2015–2022, and its re-widening in the low 20s comes from eight players. The Round 2 straight-line cone came from the log-linear trends, which kept late picks too wide.

  7. Round 1: second-order random walks on spread and skew adopted

    Needed starting points near the prior means (random starts left a chain stuck at the tree-depth limit, R-hat 1.59) and adapt_delta 0.99 (0.95 left 22–63 divergences). Clean on three seeds after that. It tied the first-order version in CV and removed the bumps.

  8. Round 2: curved trends vs per-pick random walks vs log-linear per-pick random walks

    With the starting-point fix, the per-pick random walk sampled cleanly with no divergences. It beat both trend versions on held-out log density and late-pick calibration.

  9. Round 2: second-order walk on the fringe spread rejected

    Smoothed the lower band, but lost about 23 held-out log-density points. The slot-to-slot spread differences turned out to be real and to carry over to unseen classes.

  10. Student-t fringe and an explicit minutes model on hold

    Discussed, not built. See the open items at the end.

Why it is the best so far: the numbers

  • Ties every ex-Gaussian variant on held-out RMSE (7.78) and has the smallest mean bias (−0.02).
  • Band coverage stays within a point of 80% in all three pick ranges.
  • Log density is 0.01 per player below the first-order version, which is noise; the Gaussian trails by 0.14.
  • Final fit: 0 divergences, R-hat ≤ 1.001, max Pareto-k 0.70 (it was 0.90 with first-order spread).

Why it is the best so far: basketball

  • Earlier picks are always worth more; the open question is only how much more.
  • Risk shrinks down the draft for reasons that change gradually: lottery picks go to bad teams that play them a lot, ceilings thin out, and later picks join contenders and sit.
  • A handful of careers (busts given minutes on tanking teams, late steals) should not convince the model that the low 20s are riskier than the teens. That pattern does not appear in 1996–2014.

Why the fringe spread jumps between slots

Among second-rounders who played, a slot's outcome spread tracks how many minutes its players got: Spearman ρ = 0.76 between each slot's spread and its players' average minutes (0.68 at the player level). A slot whose 4–8 players barely got on the floor has outcomes packed around zero; a slot where one or two got real minutes is wide. The held-out CV shows these slot-level differences carry over to new draft classes, which is why the smoother second-order version lost.

Why it is the best so far: the numbers

  • Best held-out log density of six models: about 12 points better than log-linear trends and 23 better than the smoothed fringe spread.
  • Best-calibrated late band (86% inside, target 80%; the trend versions sit at 89–90%).
  • Mean bias −0.07 with no divergences in CV or in the final fit (R-hat ≤ 1.001, ESS ≥ 5,208).
  • The curved trend lowers the late-pick mean more (+0.17 vs +0.25), but at a cost of 0.10 log density per player.

Why it is the best so far: basketball

  • Second-rounders really are two groups: end-of-bench players near replacement, and a few who become rotation players worth 10–25 wins.
  • The round is not one smooth slope. Early seconds still include first-round talent that slipped; after about pick 50, teams shift to draft-and-stash and two-way players, and almost nobody becomes a contributor.
  • Playing time varies a lot slot to slot, and that pattern repeats across classes, so the model is right to let neighboring slots differ.

Open items

  • Late second-round picks are still overstated by about 0.25 wins (picks 51–60), and picks 31–40 are understated by about 0.45. Both come from requiring expected value to fall smoothly.
  • Picks 1–3 are understated by about 2 wins for the same reason (the 2015–22 No. 3 picks out-produced No. 2).
  • Student-t fringe (on hold). A t-distributed fringe is a mix of narrow and wide bell curves, which mirrors the minutes story. It might let a smooth fringe spread fit as well as the current one. Worth testing as t + first-order and t + second-order spread.
  • Explicit minutes model (on hold). Modeling playing time and per-minute quality separately is the structural fix for both rounds. It needs season-level data in the model.
  • Round 2 PSIS-LOO is unreliable for the two big outliers (Pareto-k up to 1.23); the mean is checked by CV instead.
  • Not yet checked: the dashboard plot was confirmed from the export and server logs, but the Trade Machine and team tabs were not clicked through. The README pick-curve screenshot and trade case-study images are old. 05_model_validation.R still has known name drift. Nothing is committed.

How the numbers were produced

  • Cross-validation: leave one draft class out, 8 folds over 2015–2022, 2 chains × 1,000 draws per fit. Outcome quantiles use 10 simulated players per posterior draw. Scripts: 03_validation/r1_likelihood_cv.R and r2_likelihood_cv.R; results in the matching _xrapm.csv files.
  • RMSE and bias compare each held-out player's 4-yr WAR to the model's expected value for that slot. Bias is predicted minus actual.
  • Log density is the average log predictive density of held-out players; higher is better.
  • Band roughness is the mean absolute second difference of the 10th/90th percentile edges across picks.
  • Production fits use 4 chains × 2,000 draws. The dashboard export keeps 2,000 aligned simulation draws.
